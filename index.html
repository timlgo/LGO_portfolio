SELECTED WORK

Five pieces, one method

Each piece starts from a business question, applies an explicit method and ends in an artifact someone can use the next day. All figures are synthetic, built to illustrate the method; none come from a client, vendor or employer.

01 · Coverage sizing

Can I extend service hours without hiring?

A client asks to go from 9 to 14 hours of daily coverage. Instead of pricing more headcount, I built the hourly demand curve from record-level data, removed 17% of monitoring noise, and redesigned four staggered shifts.

Result: a 14-hour window with the same team, and demand coverage up from 84% to 94%. The problem was never a lack of people; it was that everyone worked the same time slot.

Synthetic monthly events by hour. The lighter bars (06:00–07:59, 17:00–20:59) held 16% of demand nobody was covering.
02 · Operating-level agreement

If a third party runs the operation, how do I keep control?

The vendor's commitment must be stricter than the one signed with the client; the difference is the management margin (for example, P1 at 3 h internally against 4 h contracted). The framework adds the five chapters most agreements leave out: volume bands, vendor continuity, labor compliance, step-in rights and return of operational data.

03 · Presales–delivery cooperation

How do I avoid selling what delivery cannot sustain?

Separate solution design (what is sold) from service design (how it runs for 24–36 months), with three gates: qualification, operability and transition. A catalog of pre-approved service patterns lets 80% of opportunities skip the engineering cycle, keeping proposals under six days.

04 · Executive dashboard

Seven indicators, three questions, one page

Are we delivering? Are we making money? Can we sustain it? The sustainability pillar (utilization and attrition) exposes what a service-only view hides: an operation buying green SLAs at the expense of its people.

05 · Service and deliverables matrix

How does a commercial catalog become verifiable obligations?

A matrix that states who executes, who is accountable and what gets delivered for every service, plus the internal deliverables nobody writes down, such as record-level operational data. It also flags the tier that looks harmless and is usually the least profitable: 24x7 reactive service with no monitoring, staffed by coverage rather than volume.

HOW I WORK

The discipline of measuring before forming an opinion

Raw data before the report
Aggregates hide exactly what needs to be seen. The data almost always exists already; nobody has pulled it.
Declare the fragile assumption
A model that does not state where it can break is not a model; it is an opinion with a table.
The artifact has to be usable
Every diagnosis ends in a template, a live model or a responsibility matrix someone can apply on Monday.
Adoption is part of the design
If a model demands capture nobody will do or governance nobody will sustain, it is badly designed, even if it is correct.
Say what did not work
Each piece includes what I would do differently. It is the only section that proves independent judgment.
Where I add the most value
  • Operations that grew without a method and do not know where they lose margin
  • Transferring an operation to a third party while keeping control and accountability
  • Multi-site or multi-country delivery that needs consistency without forced uniformity
  • Organizations that measure activity and need to start measuring outcomes
What I usually find first
  • The data exists, but no one has used it
  • The team is not the wrong size; it is in the wrong place
  • Whatever is not declared as an exclusion ends up being delivered for free
  • The green indicator hides an exhausted team
